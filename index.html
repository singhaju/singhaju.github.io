The paper

Kubernetes decides who may do what with several modules that do not know about each other, and its built-in roles can only grant access, never deny it. U-HAP writes role-based, attribute-based, access-control-list and explicit deny rules in one policy language and compiles them into one graph.

Every request then gets one clear answer, and a deny always wins. In lab tests, U-HAP matched Open Policy Agent decision for decision and applied policy updates faster.

My part

I am the second author. I co-developed the authorisation webhook and its policy model in Python, and I presented the paper at the conference in Bangkok.

The work was supervised by Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw.

The competition

TOI is the national programming olympiad of Thailand. Contestants solve algorithm problems by writing programs, and the programs are graded by computer. It is run by the POSN foundation, which promotes academic olympiads in Thailand, together with IPST, the national institute for science and technology teaching, and a university hosts each year’s contest.

TOI18 was hosted by the Faculty of Science at Chiang Mai University. According to a university news report, 15 teams with 93 students took part, and with organisers, mentors and teachers more than 300 people were there.

The road to the contest

  1. 1

    A selection at a computer centre

    Each regional POSN computer centre runs a programming and problem-solving selection that is open to every eligible student in its area.

  2. 2

    Two training camps

    The selected students go through POSN computer camp 1 and camp 2. I did both, and an IPST camp 1, in 2021 and 2022.

  3. 3

    The national contest

    Several days of contest. The current rules ask for at least two competition days of five hours each.

What it can lead to

TOI is meant to prepare students for the International Olympiad in Informatics (IOI). Under the current rules, the top 25 or so contestants are invited to a training camp at IPST, and Thailand’s representatives are chosen from there.

Medals go by rank: gold is for about one contestant in twelve, so first place is the single highest score of the whole field.

  • Training: over 1,000 hours of intensive training before the contest.
  • Teaching: since 2022 I teach olympiad programming at TOI Tutor and ABIG Center: 500+ hours, 50+ students. I wrote the C++ curriculum.

TU Alert: campus incident reports in LINE

Built for Thammasat University, launching soon

A way to report a campus incident from the app students already use, and to put it in front of the office that can act on it.

Role
Tech lead: architecture, security design and code
Built for
Thammasat University
Status

Where
Inside LINE, with a staff view

Background

A university campus is shared by many people every day, across lecture halls, dormitories, roads and open ground. Sooner or later something needs reporting: a hazard, a safety concern, a fault in a facility.

What happens next depends on one thing: whether the report reaches the office that is able to act on it.

The problem

Before TU Alert, the university had no single channel for reporting an incident to the office able to act on it. People phoned around to find the right staff, and a report that reached the wrong office stayed unresolved.

The campus it is made for

Thammasat University was founded on 27 June 1934 as the University of Moral and Political Sciences, on the ideas of Professor Dr. Pridi Banomyong. Today it has four campuses: Tha Prachan, Pattaya, Rangsit and Lampang.

TU Alert is made for the Rangsit campus, in Pathum Thani. The campus covers more than 1,757 rai, about 2.8 square kilometres. A place that large has many corners where something can go wrong, and many offices that each look after only a part of it.

From a hackathon to a commission

TU Alert began at a Thammasat University hackathon that the team attended. The university adopted the idea and commissioned the team to build it for real use.

It is built for Thammasat University and launching soon.

The approach

TU Alert meets students where they already are. It lives inside LINE, an app students already use, so nothing new has to be installed. A report takes a photo, a map pin and a few details, and the reporter can stay anonymous if they wish.

Behind the report, the system finds the right office and keeps the case moving until it is dealt with.

How a report travels

  1. 1

    Report

    A student reports inside LINE with a photo and a map pin, anonymously if they wish.

  2. 2

    Route

    The report reaches the right office by category and campus zone.

  3. 3

    Act

    The staff of that office take the case and work on it.

  4. 4

    Follow up

    The reporter follows the case until it is dealt with.

One report, from start to finish

Here is an example, not a real case. A student sees a broken light on the way to a dormitory. In LINE, the student opens TU Alert, takes a photo, drops a pin on the map and picks a category.

The alert arrives in the LINE group of the office that looks after that kind of problem in that part of the campus. One member of staff accepts the case, and from then on it is theirs: a second person cannot take it by mistake. The student watches the status move from waiting to in progress to resolved, and can ask the staff a question in the chat.

Who uses it

  • Students report, follow their own cases and chat with staff.
  • Staff work the queue of their own department and nobody else’s.
  • Department heads see the same, plus summaries of the work, and connect the department’s LINE groups so that alerts arrive where the team already talks.
  • Administrators manage the users, the departments, the categories and the routing rules.

What the design has to get right

  • Trust from the first design: a report can be sensitive, so security and privacy were designed in from the start, not added at the end.
  • Protecting reporters: people who choose to stay anonymous must be able to rely on that.
  • The right eyes only: each report should be visible only to the people who should see it.
  • Staying dependable: the system has to resist abuse such as spam and forged messages.

Rules that outrank any feature

People may report sensitive things, so a few rules come before every feature. The national ID number is never collected. A student signs in with a Thammasat account, and the password is checked and not kept. Photos are saved again without their hidden details, such as the place stored in the file. Staff sign in with a second step after the password.

A choice left out on purpose

Reports are routed by clear rules, not by a model. A wrong automatic route is worse for a student than a slightly slower human one, so the rules have to be trustworthy first. Automatic categories and a triage chatbot were left out for now.

What it should change

Once launched, anyone on campus can report from the app they already use. The right office is alerted, and the university gains a record of every incident, how quickly it was answered and whether it was followed through.

Singha is the tech lead: leading the team, owning the architecture and the security design, and writing code, from the first design choices to the code itself.

Status: built, and launching soon at Thammasat University.

AI agents for third-party risk at Allianz Ayudhya

Jun to Jul 2026In production use

Two AI agents that help people run third-party risk reviews, built during a summer internship and now in production use. A person still checks every answer.

4 June to 31 July 2026

Background

Every supplier relationship introduces risk. A supplier may hold an organisation’s data, run part of its service or connect to its systems, so the supplier’s weaknesses can become the organisation’s own.

Third-party risk management (TPRM) exists to contain that risk. Much of it runs on due-diligence questionnaires: long lists of questions about how a company protects information, which one side fills in and the other reviews.

Learning the process first

The internship ran from 4 June to 31 July 2026, as the summer training of the degree, and it began without any code. For the first weeks Singha followed how the Information Security team handles third-party risk management by hand, from the supplier side and from the buyer side, and read the documents behind each step.

The mentor was the company’s Chief Information Security Officer. From the CISO came a working view of governance and risk: how to look at a risk, what to check, what matters most and why. The CISO also explained how each department works and passed on many soft skills.

The problem

Allianz Ayudhya sits on both sides of third-party risk. As a supplier, the Information Security team fills in long due-diligence questionnaires by hand, searching internal documents for every answer. As a buyer, business owners who rarely do it must run the risk process for each new vendor.

So each review was largely manual, and many steps depended on people making the right call. The aim was to help close those gaps by supporting people’s judgement, not by replacing it.

The approach

Singha designed and built two AI agents that support the team rather than replace it. Each does the routine first pass, and a person verifies every output.

Both are now in production use by the Information Security team and by business owners.

The supplier-side agent

  • An evidence-based draft: the agent reads the company’s policy and evidence documents, matches each question to the evidence and drafts an answer, so reviewers begin from a draft rather than a blank form.
  • Every answer shows its working: each draft arrives with the evidence it used, a reason and a confidence score, so a reviewer can see at a glance how far to trust it.
  • Careful with internal information: it shows how to answer without giving away internal information.
  • Gaps found, fixes suggested: a gap analysis shows where the current controls fall short of what the questionnaire asks for, and suggests how to close each gap.
  • A person checks every answer: it only does the first pass, and it was designed that way on purpose.

The buyer-side agent

  • It teaches the process: it is tuned for business owners who are new to it, and explains each step and the key ideas so that they learn to run the process themselves.
  • It keeps track: it follows where the owner is in the process and checks their inputs and outputs at each step.
  • It warns: it points out which parts to look at closely, which to re-check or reconsider, and what could follow if something important is overlooked.
  • It supports, it does not substitute: it guides business owners; it does not do the work for them.

People stay in charge

Both agents were designed around one rule: the agent prepares and guides, and people decide.

AI is a tool, not the whole answer. In security it works best when it supports people, and the responsibility for how it is used always stays with them.

Nine weeks, step by step

The tools are internal, so no screens are shown here.

  1. 1

    Weeks 1 and 2: learn the process

    Met the Information Security team and learned third-party risk from the supplier side and the buyer side. Read sample past questionnaires to see how much manual effort each takes, and mapped the manual workflow to find where an agent helps most.

  2. 2

    Week 2: requirements

    Agreed with the team what every answer must carry: the evidence, a reason and a confidence score.

  3. 3

    Weeks 3 to 5: build the questionnaire agent

    Designed how the documents are read and how the agent reasons, then built the matching, the reasons and scores, and the gap analysis. Tested against real, anonymised cases and compared with manual answers, logged the mistakes, showed progress to the team on 26 June and refined the agent from their feedback.

  4. 4

    Week 6: demonstrate, then begin the second agent

    The questionnaire agent was demonstrated to the Information Security team on 6 July 2026. The next day the second agent began, with a study of where business owners get confused or make mistakes.

U-HAP: one place for every Kubernetes access rule

Role
Second author; co-developed the webhook; presented the paper
Venue
IEEE JCSSE 2026, Bangkok, 24 to 27 June
Award
Best Paper Award
Supervised by
Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw

Background

Kubernetes runs a large share of today’s cloud infrastructure. Every request to it, such as creating a workload or reading a secret, passes an authorisation check that decides who may do what.

There are four common ways to write the rules. Role-based access control (RBAC) grants permissions to roles. Attribute-based access control (ABAC) decides from properties of the user, the resource and the situation. Access control lists (ACLs) attach rules to a single resource. An explicit deny blocks an action whatever else allows it.

The problem

Kubernetes decides who may do what with several modules that do not know about each other, and its built-in roles can only grant access, never deny it. Organisations also need ACLs, attribute-based rules and explicit denies.

Defined separately, the four models fragment the checks, settle conflicts implicitly and slow decisions down. When different kinds of rules meet, nobody can easily say which one wins, and a small misconfiguration can quietly open an unintended gap.

The approach

U-HAP writes every kind of rule in one policy language: role-based, attribute-based, access control lists and explicit deny rules. It compiles them in advance into a single graph, and each model keeps its own meaning.

A request then becomes one question: is there a path from the subject to the resource, and does it cross a deny? The answer is served by a webhook that Kubernetes calls for each request.

How a request is answered

  1. 1

    The API server asks

    Kubernetes sends the request to the U-HAP webhook.

  2. 2

    The rules are checked in a fixed order

    Deny, then ACL, then RBAC, then ABAC, then deny by default. No allow rule can override an explicit deny, so combining models cannot create a gap.

  3. 3

    The answer comes from compiled policies

    The webhook looks the answer up in policies compiled ahead of time, instead of searching the rules each time.

Key details

  • A deny always prevails: the fixed order of evaluation means no allow rule can override an explicit deny; compilation, pruning and caching keep the cost of a decision independent of policy size.
  • Fast because the work is done early: policies are compiled into lookup tables, so a role check becomes a single bit operation and a condition shared by many rules is evaluated only once.
  • Light at run time: each request skips the rules that cannot match it, and new users need no rebuild because their attributes arrive with each request.

Evaluation

To test the idea, U-HAP was compared with Open Policy Agent (OPA) on the same policies. It matched OPA decision for decision, and it applied policy updates faster in lab benchmarks.

Role and status

Singha is the second author, co-developed the authorisation webhook and its policy model in Python, and presented the paper at IEEE JCSSE 2026 in Bangkok, 24 to 27 June. The work was supervised by Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw.

The paper won the Best Paper Award and is published in the conference proceedings. It helps platform and security teams most: one place to write and review every access rule, and a clear order of precedence that is easy to justify to an auditor.

Make conflict resolution explicit and put the safe outcome first: deny, then allow, then deny by default. It is easy to explain to an auditor and simple to test.

Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw

Background

Zero trust is the idea that no request is trusted just because of where it comes from: every request has to be checked. In the cloud that means checking who is calling and what they may do, again and again, across services that belong to different teams and, increasingly, to partner organisations.

Federated sign-in lets a person use one login across organisations, but it proves who the person is, not what they are allowed to do.

The problem

Once signed in, a user often gets the same broad access everywhere, whether the resource is harmless or highly sensitive. Because sign-in proves identity but not permissions, every request needs repeated checks, costly proofs or a blockchain call.

Stronger checks usually add delay to every request, and controls that are too slow tend to be bypassed or disabled.

The approach

FEDAC decides once. A central hub checks the caller once, at the level of assurance the service needs, and issues a short-lived pass limited to that one service.

A sidecar beside each service then checks the pass locally on every call, with no round trip to a central server and no blockchain call. The expensive verification happens once; the cheap check happens every time.

How a request is handled

  1. 1

    Check once

    The central hub verifies the caller at the level of assurance the service needs.

  2. 2

    Receive a pass

    The hub issues a short-lived pass limited to that one service.

  3. 3

    Check locally

    The sidecar beside the service checks the pass on every call, with no round trip to a central server.

  4. 4

    Renew or revoke

    Before a pass expires it is renewed, re-issued under the current policy, or the user is asked to sign in again. If a policy changes or a risk signal appears, the affected passes are revoked at once.

Key details

  • Decide once, check locally: decision-making is separated from enforcement, so the costly step runs once and every later request needs only a local check.
  • Strength that matches the risk: assurance scales across four levels of sign-in, from ordinary single sign-on up to a zero-knowledge proof. A low-risk service stays quick; a sensitive one asks for more.
  • Privacy by design: at the top level a user proves they hold the required attributes without revealing them, and each service sees a different pseudonym, so activity cannot be linked across services.
  • Governance by design: the policy set is anchored on a blockchain as a Merkle root, so any unauthorised change is detectable and rollback shows. The root is checked when a pass is issued, not on every request, so it adds no per-request delay.

Evaluation

Three published designs (Heimdall, ZKP-CapBAC and Atalay) were re-implemented and compared with FEDAC on AWS EKS. The full comparison is in the paper, which has been submitted.

Two limits are stated openly: the tests ran in a single AWS region, and the prototype signs attributes but does not yet encrypt them.

Role and status

Singha is the first author, did the research design and built the implementation in Go and Rust: the hub that issues passes, and the sidecar that checks them. The work was supervised by Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw and has been submitted to IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing (TDSC).

Who it is for

Banks, hospitals and public agencies that share services across clusters or with partners. Staff get fast access to routine services and strong verification on sensitive ones, while disclosing as little personal data as possible.

Separate the checks that must run on every request from the ones that can run once. Strong assurance and low latency can live together when the expensive verification is done once and its result travels as a short-lived pass.

Background

Large organisations run several Kubernetes clusters. When one cluster is asked to let a workload from another cluster in, a controller makes the decision and an enforcer carries it out.

The decision travels as a digitally signed answer. The signatures in use today rest on mathematics that a large quantum computer could one day break, which is why post-quantum signatures exist.

The problem

The enforcer believes the controller’s answer because it is signed. That is the weak point. A compromised controller can drop a deny rule and still return a validly signed “allow”.

And the signatures themselves are ones a quantum computer could one day forge.

The approach

PQ-SCVA-MCK changes who has to be trusted. The enforcer does not take the controller’s word: it verifies first, checking each decision against the full committed rule set, so an omitted deny rule shows up as an invalid proof.

The heavy checking happens first, and later checks stay light.

Key details

  • Distinctive: it proves the complete decision, uses post-quantum signatures and makes no blockchain call per request, where related schemes only authenticate records.
  • Holds under compromise: the guarantee holds even if workloads and authorisation components are compromised.
  • Security: it defends against omitted deny rules, stale proofs, rollback, forged capabilities and bypassed revocation.

Evaluation

The design was evaluated on AWS EKS with a live ledger, against three re-implemented schemes, and it sustains higher throughput than they do.

ZKAudit: audit cloud logs without revealing who you are

Accepted, KST 2027

An auditor proves in zero knowledge that it may audit, and a tampered log shows.

Role
First author, with Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw
Status
Accepted, KST 2027
Part of
Senior project: ZKAudit, ZK-ALBA and PQZ-Audit
Evaluated with
A Rust prototype on AWS

Background

Organisations increasingly keep their logs with a cloud provider. A log is the record of what happened in a system, such as who signed in and what changed, and an audit starts from it.

Two ideas appear in this work. A zero-knowledge proof shows that a statement is true without revealing anything more. A Merkle tree commits to a long list of records with one short value, so any record can be checked against it.

The problem

Auditing cloud logs poses a dilemma. The provider that holds the logs could alter, delete or withhold records, so the auditor cannot simply trust what it is shown.

Yet the logs are sensitive, and even who may audit has to stay private. Existing schemes verify an auditor’s right and the log’s integrity separately, or funnel every proof through a single verifier.

The approach

ZKAudit lets an auditor prove, in zero knowledge, that it holds a valid credential for the audit scope. It shows the right to audit without showing who it is.

The proof is then bound to the exact anchored log state. If the provider swaps in a different log, the proof no longer matches and the substitution is rejected.

How an audit runs

  1. 1

    Producers sign

    Log producers send signed log records to the cloud provider.

  2. 2

    The owner anchors

    The log owner registers the producers, gives the auditor a credential limited to its scope and anchors each signed epoch on a ledger.

  3. 3

    The auditor proves

    The auditor proves with a zero-knowledge proof that its request is allowed, and gets back records with Merkle proofs.

Key details

  • Right and integrity together: existing schemes check them separately; ZKAudit joins them in one step.
  • Bound to the exact log: the admission is tied to the anchored log state, so a substituted log is rejected.
  • Who audits stays private: the proof shows the right to audit, not the identity of the auditor.
  • Built for many audits: sharded, batched verifiers serve many concurrent audits.

Evaluation

ZKAudit was evaluated with a Rust prototype on AWS.

Status

Stage

Background

Cloud logs are often stored encrypted, which protects them but makes them hard to question. PQZ-Audit is the third step of the senior project and follows ZKAudit and ZK-ALBA.

Post-quantum cryptography uses algorithms designed to stay secure even against a quantum computer.

The problem

Encrypting logs protects them, but an auditor still has to ask questions of them. The usual answer is to decrypt, and that undoes the protection.

There is a quieter danger too. An attacker can store encrypted logs today and wait for a quantum computer to open them later. This is known as “harvest now, decrypt later”.

The approach

PQZ-Audit lets auditors run checks over encrypted logs, such as time windows, thresholds and totals, and then verify the answer.

It uses post-quantum cryptography throughout, so an attacker who stores the logs today gains nothing from a future quantum computer.

Key details

  • Checks on encrypted logs: time windows, thresholds and totals, verified without seeing the logs.
  • Completeness: epochs are committed and anchored on a permissioned ledger, and zero-knowledge proofs show that skipped blocks could not match.
  • Totals without plaintext: homomorphic encryption is used only for totals.
  • Shared authority: threshold committees keep one authority from both authorising an audit and recovering the data; privileged audits and disclosure need approval from t of n members.

PQZ-Audit is the third step of the senior project, written as first author with Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw. The design is written, and experiments and submission are next.

MoCA: containing AI agents after a prompt injection succeeds

Assume the injection works, and make sure that success cannot become authority.

Role
First author, with Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw
Status
In preparation, journal paper
Began as

Topic
AI-agent security

Background

An AI agent is a language model that can act: it calls tools, reads documents and web pages, and works with real credentials on a person’s behalf. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) is a common way for an agent to reach those tools and data.

The model decides what to do from the text in front of it, and that is where the risk begins.

The problem

A retrieved web page or a tampered tool description can contain instructions of its own, and the agent may follow them. This is prompt injection, and it is hard to filter out completely.

The danger is that a trick played on the model turns into actions done with the user’s own authority.

The approach

MoCA starts from the opposite assumption: an injection will succeed some day, and the design must make sure that this success cannot become authority.

The model is treated as an untrusted black box. It may suggest anything, but every action it asks for is checked at an MCP gateway and again at the resource itself.

Key details

  • Delegated authority: the user signs a delegation capped at their own rights, so an agent can never do more than its user could.
  • A committed tool manifest: a silently altered tool is rejected.
  • Trust drops on untrusted text: reading untrusted text lowers the context’s trust, which withdraws the authority to act on it.
  • Two checks: every action is checked at the MCP gateway and again at the resource.

MoCA began as a project in the Cyber Crimes and Digital Forensics course. It is now a journal paper in preparation, written as first author with Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw.

SWARM-Fog: self-healing orchestration for industrial IoT

Submitted

Fog clusters that schedule, balance and recover as one system, and learn from each other.

Role
First author, with Prof. Somchart Fugkeaw
Status
Submitted
Setting
Industrial IoT fog networks
Tested on
A controlled testbed on AWS EC2, with software-emulated enclaves

Background

In industrial settings, machines and sensors send their data to small clusters of computers placed close by, in what is called a fog layer between the devices and the cloud. These clusters decide where each piece of work should run.

Factories cannot wait for a distant data centre, so the clusters have to schedule, balance and recover from failures by themselves.

The problem

Industrial fog networks place work from instantaneous local readings, so when work moves it can pile up on one node and create a hotspot.

Recovery waits on cluster leaders, and what one cluster learns is never reused by another, so each starts from nothing.

The approach

SWARM-Fog treats scheduling, balancing and recovery as one problem instead of three. The clusters share a fading “pheromone” map of what worked, much as ants leave trails that grow stale unless they are refreshed.

A cluster that has found a good placement leaves a mark, and its neighbours can follow it. Marks that stop being useful fade away.

Key details

  • No fixed controller: each cluster has a temporary, replaceable coordinator that cannot seize permanent control.
  • Trust-aware scheduling: work is placed with the trust of each node in mind.
  • Plans before failure: recovery plans are prepared before a node fails.
  • Shared memory: a fading map of what worked lets one cluster reuse what another learned.

Evaluation

On a controlled testbed on AWS EC2, with software-emulated enclaves, the system recovered fully across a wide range of failure rates. It was faster than four re-implemented baselines at equal completeness.
