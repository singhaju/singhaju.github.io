AI agents for third-party risk at Allianz Ayudhya

Two AI agents for third-party risk management that support people's judgement, built in the Information Security team.

Why it matters

Before engaging a new supplier, a company must assess that supplier's security, and a large company also receives the same kind of questionnaires from its own customers. Much of this work is manual, and many steps depend on sound human judgement.

Motivation

I wanted to help close the gaps in the process, not by replacing people, but by supporting their judgement.

Context

Information security internship at Allianz Ayudhya (4 June to 31 July 2026, university summer training). I was mentored by the Chief Information Security Officer, Apichai Wongchindakhun, and worked with the Information Security team. I spent the first weeks learning the team's manual third-party risk management (TPRM) process, from the supplier side and from the buyer side.

Outcome

  • Questionnaire agent demonstrated, 6 Jul 2026

Built in a two-month internship. The screens are internal to Allianz Ayudhya, so none are shown.

What I learned

From my mentor, the CISO, I learned how information security, governance and risk management work in practice: how to look at risk, what to check, what matters most and why. I followed the process from start to end on real, anonymised cases and read many documents to understand it. He also taught me how each department works, along with many soft skills.

The problem

Allianz Ayudhya sits on both sides of third-party risk. As a supplier, the Information Security team fills in long due-diligence questionnaires by hand, searching internal documents for every answer. As a buyer, business owners who rarely do it must run the risk process for each new vendor. So the process still has gaps: much of it is done by hand, and many steps depend on people making the right call. I wanted to help close those gaps by supporting people's judgement, not replacing it.

Agent 1: questionnaires (supplier side)

It takes the first pass at a questionnaire. It reads the company's policy and evidence documents, matches each question to the evidence and drafts the answer. Every answer comes with the evidence it used, a reason and a confidence score, and shows how to answer without giving away internal information. A gap analysis shows where current controls fall short and suggests how to close each gap, and a fit-gap analysis compares current controls with what the questionnaire asks for. It only does the first job: a person checks every answer, and I designed it that way on purpose.

Agent 2: guidance (buyer side)

For business owners who are new to the process. It does not just help them fill in information: it teaches them to run the process themselves, explains each step and the key ideas, keeps track of where they are, checks their inputs and outputs, and warns them about anything important they should not miss. It supports them; it does not do the work for them.

How I worked

First the requirements, with the team; then the design (how the agents read the documents, the prompts and the reasoning flow); then the build; then tests against real, anonymised cases. I showed my progress to the team often and refined both agents from their feedback. I demonstrated the questionnaire agent on 6 July 2026; after end-to-end tests and documentation, I presented both agents on 31 July 2026.

The lesson

AI is a tool, not the whole answer. In security, it is at its best when it supports people, not when it replaces their thinking. And the responsibility for how we use it always stays with us.

Who benefits

Security reviewers, who verify a drafted answer instead of writing each one from scratch; and business owners, who run a vendor review only a few times a year and need clear guidance.
